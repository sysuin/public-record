Is Delhi's air getting worse?

We read all 4,218 daily air-quality bulletins the Central Pollution Control Board has published since 2015. By the government's own number, Delhi's autumn air is not getting worse. This year's improvement arrived in the same weeks the city's monitor count rose by more than a quarter, and the bulletins cannot separate the two.

The answer everyone assumes

Every November the same headline returns: Delhi's air has hit a new low. The working assumption, repeated every year, is that the city is getting worse. We tested it against the one number the government publishes for every city, every day.

The source

At 4 pm each day the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) publishes a PDF with an air quality score for every city it monitors. The score runs from 0 to 500. Above 200 is Poor, above 300 Very Poor, above 400 Severe. Each bulletin sits at a predictable address, so anyone can check a day for themselves:

https://www.cpcb.gov.in/upload/Downloads/AQI_Bulletin_20261120.pdf

We downloaded every bulletin from May 2015 to 20 November 2026 (4,218 files; 35 days were never published) and read the rows for Delhi, Gurugram, Noida, Ghaziabad, Faridabad and Greater Noida. To compare an unfinished winter fairly, every year below uses the same dates: 1 October to 20 November.

What the number says

Delhi's worst autumns in the record are 2016 and 2017, averaging 320 and 321. This year's average so far is 226, the lowest of the eleven. Matched day for day against 2016–2025, it is about a fifth lower (-21%, with a plausible range of -33% to -12% given how much single days swing).

The worst days have eased faster than the typical day. Delhi had 12 Severe days in this window in 2016. This year it has had none, and so have Gurugram, Noida, Ghaziabad, Faridabad and Greater Noida. Every earlier year had at least one NCR city above 400. No day in the whole archive has reached 500; Delhi's highest reading is 497, so a "record" on this scale would need a number the bulletin has never printed.

None of this makes the air clean. Between 1 and 20 November this year, 20 of 20 days were Poor or worse. "Not getting worse" describes the direction, not the level.

The turn: the number is not counting the same thing

Delhi's figure in the bulletin is an average of whichever monitoring stations reported that day. CPCB says plainly on its own bulletin that averaging stations this way is not scientifically sound, and it still prints the number.

That average has been built from very different networks. In autumn 2016 it came from about 6 stations. From 2019 to 2025 it came from 33 to 40. Then, between 7 and 13 October this year, in the middle of the comparison window, the listed count jumped from 46 to 59.

Neighbouring cities expanded too, and not evenly. Compare each city's 1–20 November average with the same dates last year, and the split lines up with the monitors:

Greater Noida (-26%), Ghaziabad (-24%), Noida (-20%) and Delhi (-10%) all added monitors and all improved. Gurugram (+7%) and Faridabad (+2%) added none and did not. For the three Uttar Pradesh cities, the gap from Gurugram this year is wider than in any earlier year.

This is a coincidence in timing that we cannot yet explain away. The cities that expanded are Delhi and the three east of it; the two that did not are the Haryana cities to the south-west, so a regional weather difference would produce the same split. Delhi's score also did not drop on the days its new stations appeared. Settling it needs station-by-station data, which CPCB keeps behind a CAPTCHA. We did not try to get past it.

The fires fell. November did not move.

Farm fires in Punjab and Haryana counted by satellite (ICAR-IARI's CREAMS bulletins, season to 20 November) fell from 83,394 in 2020 to 5,638 in 2025, a drop of 93%. Over the same years Delhi's 1–20 November average went from 349 to 355.

A satellite only sees a fire that is burning when it passes overhead. This data cannot tell "farm smoke matters less than the headlines say" from "more burning now happens when no satellite is looking". Both fit the numbers.

The corrected answer

Hold the network steady and the trend disappears. From 2019 to 2025, when Delhi's score came from a stable 33 to 40 stations, the autumn average moved by less than one point a year (-0.04, plausible range -9 to +9). The whole-year average stayed between 185 and 215.

Weather explains most of what changes from one autumn to the next. Taking out wind, rain, temperature, humidity and the date of Diwali removes roughly three-quarters of the difference between years. The "good" autumns of 2021 and 2022 were the two wet ones, with 119 mm and 132 mm of rain between October and mid-November. After the weather, every autumn from 2016 to 2025 lands between 97 and 111 on a scale where 100 is typical.

This year still looks better after the weather, at about 84 (range 74–95). It also arrived in the same month the monitor count jumped, and with weather taken from a different NASA model than earlier years.

By the government's own number, Delhi's autumn air is not getting worse. It has not clearly been getting better either. For seven years it has been stuck, rising and falling with the weather.

What this data cannot tell you

  • Whether this year's improvement is real. Only a recount on last year's stations would show it, and station data sits behind a CAPTCHA.
  • How much pollution anyone breathed. The bulletin is a 0–500 index for a city average, not a measure of dust in micrograms or of what one street experienced.
  • Why the fires and the air came apart. Two explanations fit equally well.
  • How precise the weather adjustment is. It is a model built on one NASA grid point, not on IMD observations, and the ranges are wide.
  • The rest of this winter. Everything here stops on 20 November. December and January decide the season.

How we did it

Source. CPCB daily AQI bulletins, 4,218 PDFs, 1 May 2015 to 20 November 2026, downloaded on 21 November 2026 from cpcb.gov.in/upload/Downloads/. Each city row gives the score, the main pollutant and the number of stations reporting.

Checks. Every bulletin was read by two independent methods; the scores agree on every one of the 20,859 city-days both could read. Six randomly chosen bulletins were checked again directly against the PDF text. 20 rows were dropped (for example, a bulletin whose header date differs from its file date) and every exclusion is logged.

Comparisons. Same calendar window every year. Ranges come from resampling days and years, so a figure is only called a change when it survives that.

Also used. ICAR-IARI CREAMS daily fire bulletins; NASA FIRMS satellite fire detections (as a cross-check, which tracks CREAMS closely); NASA MERRA-2 and GEOS-IT weather. IMD observations need an account, so we did not use them.

Reproduce it. Scripts, derived data, the full research file and the exclusion log: github.com/sysuin/public-record/tree/main/r01-delhi-air. Every number on this page is read from them at build time.
