Does one T20 season predict the next?

We followed every player through 9,612 T20 matches across 32 competitions, 2.27 million balls in all. Taken at face value, a player's last season is a worse guide to his next one than assuming he is exactly average. Here is what works instead, in time for this month's IPL auction.

The answer everyone assumes

Every December, franchises spend crores on the strength of the season just gone. A batter who struck at 180 is in form; a bowler who went at six an over is a find. The assumption underneath is simple: what a player did last season is roughly what he will do next season. We tested how much of a season actually carries over.

The source

Cricsheet, a long-running volunteer project, transcribes every ball of every match from the official scorecards and publishes them free at cricsheet.org/downloads. We downloaded every T20 competition it covers on 21 November 2026: 14,419 matches. After removing rain-shortened, abandoned and reduced-overs games, and internationals involving associate nations, 9,612 matches and 2,267,624 deliveries remain: the IPL, Big Bash, PSL, CPL, SA20, ILT20, the Blast, Syed Mushtaq Ali and ten more men's competitions, ten women's competitions, The Hundred, and internationals between full members.

The leagues publish scorecards one match at a time. Cricsheet is the only place they exist as one download, so it stands in for the official record here, and 386 matches it has not released are missing.

Every number in this report compares a player with the average of his league in that season. A strike rate of 150 in a league that scored at 140 counts as +10. Without that step, league-wide scoring rises (the IPL's in 2023–26, for example) make players look more consistent than they are.

What the obvious numbers say

Take every batter who faced at least 120 balls in two seasons running in the same men's league (2,167 pairs) and put this season's strike rate next to the next one. Last season explains about a tenth of next season. For economy rate it is about a sixth, for wicket-taking under 4 per cent, and for batting average under 3 per cent. The IPL looks like every other league.

What repeats strongly is the job. Whether a bowler is used in the powerplay repeats at 0.80; how well he bowls repeats at 0.40, and only 0.29 once you allow for the overs he is given.

The turn: a season is mostly luck

Split one season into a player's odd and even matches and the two halves barely agree. The reason is size. A season is small, and a few big innings or one bad over move the numbers a long way.

A strike rate needs about 270 balls before it is more skill than luck, and a regular faces about 220 in a season, so a full season's strike rate is still roughly half noise. Boundary percentage settles faster, at about 170 balls. A wicket rate needs about 1,340 balls, roughly six seasons of a regular bowler, and a batting average about 1,640, seven or eight seasons.

So the best seasons fall back hardest. The top fifth of strike-rate seasons were 31 runs per 100 balls above their league's average. The next year the same players were 12 above.

Breakout batters, whose season beat their own career strike rate by 27 on average, kept 35% of the jump. Breakout bowlers kept 24%.

The corrected answer

We tested five ways of predicting the next season. Every setting was chosen on 2008–2019 and then scored on 2020–2026, seasons the methods had never seen.

  • Last season at face value is the worst option tested. It adds 43% more error than assuming every player is league average for strike rate, 32% for economy and 92% for wicket rate.
  • What works is weighting recent seasons more and pulling every number toward the average. That beats "everyone is average" by 13% for strike rate, 24% for economy and 20% for boundary percentage, and it beats last season at face value in all 60 combinations of measure, league group and test period we ran.
  • It holds in the IPL alone (strike rate −36% → +11%; economy −23% → +32%), in women's leagues (−58% → +17%), and when the test is run backwards (−53% → +12%).

Two more findings matter for an auction room. Batters arriving from the Syed Mushtaq Ali Trophy were 19 runs per 100 balls above its average and level with the IPL average once they got there (93 player-seasons), and Mushtaq Ali economy rates showed almost no link to IPL ones (0.09). Form in overseas leagues was at least as good a guide to an IPL season (0.35) as the IPL's own previous season (0.24); the ranges overlap, so "no worse" is the safe reading.

Last season is evidence, not a forecast. Pull it hard toward the average, add earlier seasons and other leagues, and buy the skills that repeat.

What this means at the auction table

  1. Never price last season at face value. On this evidence it is worse than pricing everyone at league average.
  2. Pull every number toward the league average, by balls. Add roughly 400 balls of average batting to a strike rate and about 300 balls of average bowling to an economy rate. A batter 30 runs per 100 balls above average in 250 balls, with no other history, projects to about +12 next season.
  3. Use earlier seasons too, leaning on the latest. Each season back counts roughly two-thirds to four-fifths as much as the one after it.
  4. Buy what repeats. Boundary percentage, dot balls, economy and a player's role carry over. One season's wicket haul or batting average is close to noise.
  5. Discount domestic strike rates by roughly 19 points before comparing them with IPL numbers, and be most suspicious of the breakout season.

What this data cannot tell you

  • Why a season went up or down. Injury, a new role, a new coach and a different home ground are all invisible here.
  • Pace against spin. The scorecard does not record bowling type.
  • Age. There are no birth dates in the data, so we drew no age curve.
  • Fielding and keeping. Not measured.
  • The players who were not picked again. Only 44% of the worst fifth of strike-rate seasons were followed by another full season, against about 54% of the rest, so every comparison describes players who came back.
  • Prices. Cricsheet has no auction data. This says what a season is worth as evidence, not whether any team overpaid.
  • Which overseas league is the best guide to the IPL. The samples are too small to rank them.

How we did it

Source. Cricsheet ball-by-ball JSON for all 32 T20 competitions it covers, downloaded 21 November 2026 (14,419 files). Seasons come from match dates. Rain-affected, shortened, abandoned and super-over-only results were removed, and every exclusion is logged.

Measures. Each player-season is compared with the average of that competition in that season. Players need 120+ balls in both seasons for the season-to-season comparisons (results checked at 30 to 400 balls).

Ranges. Every figure was re-run on 1,000 reshuffled sets of players, so a result is only reported when it survives that.

Forecasts. Settings chosen on 2008–2019 targets only, scored on 2020–2026, then run in reverse.

Checks. Every headline number was computed a second time by independent code. That second pass caught one real bug (dismissals off wides were missing), which is fixed.

Reproduce it. Scripts, derived data, the research file and the exclusion log: github.com/sysuin/public-record/tree/main/r02-t20-repeatability. Every number on this page is read from them at build time.
