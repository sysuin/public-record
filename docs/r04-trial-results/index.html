Do Clinical Trials Report Results?
Public Record · Sartium

How many clinical trials actually publish their results?

We downloaded all 612,892 records on ClinicalTrials.gov, the US government's trial registry. Most trials there have no results, but most of those were never required to post them there. Among trials US law probably covers, nearly 9 in 10 eventually report.

The answer everyone assumes

"Most clinical trials never report their results" is one of medicine's best-known complaints. The campaign figure that half of all trials go unreported dates from 2013 and is about publication in journals. On the US government's own trial registry, the number looks even worse.

The source

ClinicalTrials.gov is run by the US National Library of Medicine. Every trial record can be downloaded free through its official API at clinicaltrials.gov/api/v2/studies. Each record gives the trial's status, the dates it started and finished, who ran it and where, whether the product is regulated by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), and the dates results were first sent in and first posted. We took all 612,892 records, 467,733 of them interventional trials, which test a treatment, device or procedure on people.

The registry stamps its own dates, such as when results arrived. Everything else in a record, including status, dates, phase, sponsor type and the FDA tick-boxes, is typed in by the sponsor.

What the obvious number says

Take completed interventional trials that finished collecting their main result between 2008 and 2023. 31% have results on ClinicalTrials.gov: 59,765 of 195,425. Here "results" means posted, or sent in and waiting to be posted; counting posted results only, it is 28%. And the share is falling: from 40.5% for trials that finished in 2008 to 24.9% for 2023.

On its face, about 7 in 10 trials never report, and it is getting worse.

The turn: most trials were never required to report here

US law covers a minority of trials. The FDA Amendments Act of 2007 (section 801) and its 2017 Final Rule require results within 12 months of a trial's primary completion for a defined group: interventional trials of FDA-regulated drugs, biologics or devices, beyond phase 1 and not small device feasibility studies, with a US site or a US-made exported product (or run under an FDA application), finishing from 18 January 2017 on.

Using only the registry's own fields, we estimated which trials are probably required to report. This is our estimate, not a legal ruling. Of the 139,674 trials that finished between 18 January 2017 and 20 November 2024, so that every one has had at least two full years, 21,041 (15%) fall in that group.

Those trials mostly report. The rest mostly don't.

Within two years, 70% of probably-required trials have results against 14% of everything else: a gap of 56 points (95% range 55.4 to 56.7), about 5 times the rate. The gap holds however we draw the line. Under stricter and looser definitions the required group's two-year figure stays between 69% and 71%, and everything else between 11% and 16%.

Most of the missing results come from trials run outside the US. Trials run only outside the US, which the law does not cover, are 58% of the trials and 70% of those with no results two years after finishing. The probably-required trials are 15% of the trials and 5.9% of the missing results.

University and hospital trials run only outside the US are, on their own, 45% of the trials and 56% of the missing results. Only 3.6% of them report here within two years. Many may report in a journal or in another country's registry, and this data cannot see that.

The "getting worse" trend is mostly a change in which trials are registered. Trials run only outside the US went from 44% of completed trials in 2008 to 66% in 2023. Trials with at least one US site have reported at a steady 53–57% every year.

Had the 2008 mix of US and non-US trials held, the 2023 figure would be 32.3%, not 24.9%. The changing mix explains about half of the fall. The other half is real: trials run only outside the US post here less and less, from 23% in 2008 to 8% in 2023.

Nearly a quarter of the missing results sit in records nobody keeps up to date. Of the 312,374 trials at least two years past their end date, 18% are in "unknown status": the label the registry applies when a record passes its completion date and nobody confirms its status for two years. They are 23% of the trials with no results. Their records were last touched a median 6.7 years ago, and only 4.5% have results. 24% of trials run only outside the US end up "unknown", against 4.5% of trials with a US site.

Inside the required group: the deadline and the stragglers

About 4 in 10 report by the legal deadline. Of the probably-required trials, 42% sent results within 12 months. Allowing 30 days of grace, 48%. Counting the delay certificates the law allows, filed in time, 51%. Results then keep arriving: for trials that finished in 2018–2022, 40% had reported at 12 months, 68% at two years, 80% at three and 88% by now.

Reports bunch at the deadline: among trials that reported within three years, the median gap between finishing and sending results was 366 days.

The overdue trials sit with sponsors who run very few trials. 2,662 probably-required trials have no results and no delay certificate. Sponsors with one to four such trials hold 73% of them, while running 31% of the trials. The 35 sponsors with 100 or more run 27% of the trials and hold 0.8% of the overdue ones (22 trials). Every one of those 35 has now reported at least 94% of its probably-required trials.

Sponsor size is counted by the name on the record. Companies that register under several names look smaller than they are, so the exact shares are approximate; the direction is not.

At the deadline, industry and universities tie. Comparing them as if they ran the same mix of trials, industry reported 42% within 12 months and universities and hospitals 42%, a difference of +0.4 points (95% range -0.9 to +1.7). Universities are ahead at two years (74% against 65%). Industry leads only once delay certificates count: it files them on 17% of these trials, universities on 3%. The NIH, with 533 trials, is fastest at 12 months (51%).

The corrected answer

  • For trials US law probably covers: about 4 in 10 report by the deadline, 7 in 10 within two years and nearly 9 in 10 eventually. Counting legal delay certificates, about half are on time.
  • For everything else on the registry: fewer than 1 in 5 report here. Most of those trials were never required to.

Four popular claims did not survive:

  • "Reporting is getting worse." About half of the fall is a change in which trials are registered. Trials with a US site have held at 53–57% for 15 years.
  • "Big pharma is the problem." The 35 largest sponsors hold 0.8% of the overdue required trials.
  • "Industry reports best." It ties universities at the deadline and trails them at two years.
  • "The 2017 rule transformed reporting." On-time reporting for drug and device trials with a US site rose from 26.5% (finished 2014–16) to 36.9% (2018–20), but it had already been rising by 2–3 points a year since 2011. That fits a modest effect and proves none.

Most trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov have no results there, but most of those were never required to post them there. Among trials US law probably covers, nearly 9 in 10 eventually report, though only about 4 in 10 by the legal deadline.

What this data cannot tell you

  • Whether a trial was published anywhere else. It may be in a journal, the EU trials register or another country's registry. "No results on ClinicalTrials.gov" does not mean "never published".
  • Who was legally required. Our group rests on sponsors' own tick-boxes. FDA application numbers are not public, and older records lack the FDA questions altogether. It is an estimate, not a compliance ruling about any trial or sponsor.
  • Whether the records are true. Status, dates, phase and sponsor type are typed in by sponsors. "Unknown status" is the registry saying it doesn't know.
  • Whether posted results are complete or correct.
  • Trials that were never registered.
  • Why small sponsors and non-US trials post less here: no obligation, another registry, or no capacity.
  • The latest years. Trials whose records still show only a planned finish date drop out of the counts. Adding back 1,954 probably-required trials stuck with a past planned date lowers the group's two-year figure from 70% to 64%.

How we did it

Source. All 612,892 records from the ClinicalTrials.gov API (version 2.0.5), downloaded on 22 November 2026; the registry's data timestamp, 20 November 2026, was the same at the start and end of the download.

Outcome. "Results" means summary results posted, or submitted and waiting in the registry's quality-control review (10,458 interventional trials are in that queue). The law counts submission, not posting.

"Probably required". Interventional, not withdrawn; the sponsor ticked "FDA-regulated drug" or "device"; not phase 1 alone; not a device feasibility study; at least one US site or a US-exported product. Three other definitions (stricter, looser, and without the location test) are reported as ranges.

Fair comparisons. Group comparisons use trials that finished between 18 January 2017 and 20 November 2024, so every trial has had two full years, and fixed windows (12 months, 2 years). Ranges are 95% intervals. Sponsor comparisons are standardised to the same mix of phase, product, year and US site.

Checks. A second, independent code path recomputed 113 headline numbers from the raw download with no mismatches, and 47 counts were checked against the registry's own search engine. We recomputed the main figures a third time before publishing.

Reproduce it. Scripts, derived data, the research file and the exclusion log: github.com/sysuin/public-record/tree/main/r04-trial-results. Every number on this page is read from them at build time.
